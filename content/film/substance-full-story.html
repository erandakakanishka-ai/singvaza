This is the film nobody in 2024 expected to become a body-horror landmark and the one everybody was still arguing about a year later. Demi Moore plays a woman the industry has decided is finished. The film then asks the question everyone is too polite to answer out loud: how far would you go to be looked at again? What follows is the entire story, scene by scene, ending included.

What everyone knows before they press play

Elisabeth Sparkle won an Oscar once. Now she hosts a morning aerobics show for a channel run by a man named Harvey (Dennis Quaid), who eats shrimp like an animal and fires her on her fiftieth birthday because the network wants someone younger and tighter. On the way home her car is smashed, she is wheeled into a hospital, and a young doctor hands her a card. On it: THE SUBSTANCE, and a phone number, and one promise — take it and a better version of yourself is born.

The story, start to end

1. The injection

She thinks about it for a week. Then she orders the box: a single-use kit, a syringe of neon-green fluid, a spinal needle, an IV line. She injects herself and convulses on her bathroom floor. From a slit down her back — the camera does not look away — something climbs out. Sue (Margaret Qualley) is young, blonde, and perfect, and she stands in front of the mirror for a long time.

The rules are stated plainly and the film will punish every one of them: you are one. They share a body: seven days for Sue, then Sue must return and stabilise — feed the body, take the fluid from Elisabeth's spine — or both of them die.

2. Sue becomes the show

Sue auditions for the same network and Harvey worships her. She becomes the new face of the aerobics show, then the star of a fitness empire. Elisabeth, meanwhile, sits in a dark apartment with a giant portrait of herself on the wall, and eats. The first time Sue ignores the clock and stays out for an extra day, she pulls a little extra fluid from Elisabeth's spine — just a bit — and a fingernail on Elisabeth's hand turns black and falls off.

Margaret Qualley as Sue. Photo: MUBI / Working Title

3. The spiral

Elisabeth starts drinking, starts hating the body she wakes up in, and starts hating Sue at the same time — which is, of course, hating herself. Sue, in turn, treats Elisabeth's turns as wasted time: the older body eats badly, stays in the flat, refuses to go out. The rules break down quietly, then completely. Sue feeds off Elisabeth without giving back. Elisabeth tries to stop the whole thing and cannot, because stopping means killing them both.

The film is honest about why this is happening: the two of them are the same woman, and the younger one already has everything she gave up.

4. Monster

They turn on each other in the apartment and destroy each other's body — Sue beating Elisabeth's spine, Elisabeth waking up in Sue's body and tearing it apart in front of a mirror. The stabilising fluid is used up, then overused, and the balance the instructions almost insisted on is gone. What is left, after one final self-injection that the kit was never designed for, is Monstro Elisasue: a towering, weeping, tissue-wrapped figure with two faces, a spine of bone down its back, and Demi Moore's eyes.

Monstro Elisasue, designed by Pierre-Olivier Persin. Photo: MUBI / Working Title

5. The ending (this is the part people argue about)

Monstro Elisasue dresses up and goes to the New Year's Eve broadcast of the show — the one Harvey has been building for Sue all year. It walks on stage in a sequinned gown, and the audience goes from laughter to total hysteria. Something snaps inside it. It sprays the crowd with blood, the room is painted red, and then the stage security tears it apart.

The last shot is the film's cruellest joke and its gentlest one: the lump of the creature that survives crawls out of the theatre and dies on the Walk of Fame, and the next morning it turns into a puddle of glitter and gore that a street sweeper quietly hoses away. Elisabeth was a star. It is a stain on a pavement, and then it is nothing.

What the film is actually saying

  • You are one. That line is the whole philosophy. The two women never had separate interests; the younger one only looked like an escape route.
  • The industry is the monster's mother. Harvey is grotesque on purpose — every frame of him is filmed like a carcass being sold.
  • Addiction. The fluid is a drug, the seven-day rule is a dose ladder, and the ending is what happens when someone with a supply cannot say no.
  • Watching. The camera is as cruel as the audience in the film. That is not an accident, and it is why the film still gets under people's skin.

Questions everyone asks after watching

Did Elisabeth die earlier? No. The film is clear that they are one body split down the middle; every switch is a semi-death, and the monster is the bill for all of them.

Why does the blood-spray scene exist? Because the film refuses to end on dignity. The audience laughs at a woman who was once famous for being looked at, so the film makes them be sprayed by it.

Is there a second film? No, and it does not need one.
