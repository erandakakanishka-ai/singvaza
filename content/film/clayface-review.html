DC has spent years selling us gods in capes. Clayface is about a man whose face falls off. It is 18-rated, it is small, it is mean, and it is — by a distance — the strangest thing Warner Bros. has put its logo on this year. I went in expecting a comic-book movie. I came out having watched a body-horror film that happens to wear one.

Tom Rhys Harries plays Matt Hagen, a star whose face becomes the story. Photo: Warner Bros. Pictures

The short version (no spoilers)

An actor at the exact moment his career takes off is beaten so badly that his face has to be rebuilt. The science that fixes him works. That is the problem. What follows is intimate, gross and genuinely sad — three things a superhero-adjacent film rarely manages all at once.

The story, without giving anything away

Matt Hagen (Tom Rhys Harries) is a jobbing actor who gets his break, books the kind of role that changes a career, and then loses everything in a single night of violence. A crime boss makes sure of it — and the damage is to his face, which for an actor is the whole business.

Dr Caitlyn Corr (Naomi Ackie) offers him a way back: an experimental treatment that rebuilds what was destroyed. It works better than anyone dared hope. It also does something to him that no one in that clinic tested for, and the film spends its second half watching a man slowly stop being able to trust his own reflection.

That is all I will say about plot. There are no spoilers in this review — the last act is worth meeting cold.

What works

  • Tom Rhys Harries. He has the hardest job in the film: play a handsome man, then play what is left of him, and keep the audience on both sides. He does it without begging for sympathy, which makes it land harder.
  • The practical effects. This is the rare modern blockbuster where you can feel that people built something physical and then pointed a camera at it. The gore is used like punctuation, not decoration.
  • The scale. Almost nobody in this film has a plan to save the world. It is a story about one man, one doctor and one relationship, and the smallness is a relief after a decade of world-ending third acts.
  • Naomi Ackie. She is the only person in the film arguing with herself, and it gives all the wet, gooey horror a conscience.

The first half plays like a drama about an actor's body. Photo: Warner Bros. Pictures

What does not work

  • The bad guys are thin. Great actors (Eddie Marsan, Max Minghella, David Dencik) are given very little to do besides stand in a room and be sinister. You feel the talent, not the characters.
  • The love story is rushed. Caitlyn and Matt need a history the film never quite earns, so the emotional payoff in the final stretch lands softer than it should.
  • It knows what it is — sometimes too well. The film leans hard on "this is a nasty little B-movie" charm. Most of the time that is fun; twice it is an excuse.

What critics are saying

Reviews have been split but mostly kind. According to Rotten Tomatoes, the Tomatometer sat around 75% from 83 reviews at release, with the audience score far higher. Variety and The Hollywood Reporter both filed positive notices praising its nerve; IndieWire and Rolling Stone were much colder, calling it an ambitious misfire. That divide is exactly right for this film — it is a small, unpleasant, well-made thing, and how much you enjoy it depends entirely on whether you want that on a Friday night.

Who should watch it

  • ✅ You liked The Fly, Possessor or anything where the monster is a person.
  • ✅ You are tired of comic-book films that look and sound identical.
  • ✅ You want to see Naomi Ackie given a real part again.
  • ❌ You are squeamish. There are scenes here that people in my screening watched through their fingers.
  • ❌ You are going with children. It is an 18 for a reason.

Final word

Clayface is the first DC film in years that feels like it was made by a person rather than a committee, and the best compliment I can give it is that it does not care whether you like it. Go for the effects, stay for Tom Rhys Harries, and don't eat beforehand.

Score: 7.5 / 10 — worth a ticket, not worth taking your mother.
